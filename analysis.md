# Analysis of Survey Results

## 1. Participant Demographics

### Q1. Which class are you currently studying in?

- **Class 11:** 28 students (43.1%)
- **Class 12:** 36 students (55.4%)
- **BSc 1st Year:** 1 student (1.5%)

**Observation:** Most respondents were HSC students, with Class 12 students forming the largest group.

**Interpretation:** The findings primarily reflect the experiences of HSC students currently studying Calculus.

### Q2. Did you choose Higher Mathematics as a subject in college?

- **Yes:** 63 students (96.9%)
- **No:** 2 students (3.1%)

**Observation:** Nearly all respondents had chosen Higher Mathematics.

**Interpretation:** The survey responses come mainly from students with direct experience studying Calculus.

### Q3. Have you studied Calculus yet?

- **Completed the Calculus chapters in the syllabus:** 30 students (46.2%)
- **Currently studying Calculus:** 35 students (53.8%)

**Observation:** Respondents were almost evenly divided between students who had completed Calculus and those who were still studying it.

**Interpretation:** The survey captures perspectives from students at different stages of learning Calculus. However, since most respondents were still studying or had recently studied the topic, the findings primarily reflect their current educational experiences.

## 2. Perceived Difficulty and Study Habits

### Q4. How difficult do you find Calculus?

- **Rating 1:** 1 student (1.5%)
- **Rating 2:** 7 students (10.8%)
- **Rating 3:** 20 students (30.8%)
- **Rating 4:** 26 students (40.0%)
- **Rating 5:** 11 students (16.9%)

**Observation:** More than half of the respondents (56.9%) rated Calculus as difficult or very difficult (ratings 4 or 5).

**Interpretation:** Calculus is perceived as challenging by a substantial proportion of the respondents. Only 12.3% gave ratings of 1 or 2, suggesting that relatively few respondents considered the subject easy.

### Q5. How often do you practice Calculus problems outside class?

- **5+ days a week:** 15 students (23.1%)
- **3–4 days a week:** 35 students (53.8%)
- **1–2 days a week:** 11 students (16.9%)
- **Less than once a week:** 3 students (4.6%)
- **Never:** 1 student (1.5%)

**Observation:** Most students reported practicing Calculus regularly, with 53.8% practicing 3–4 days per week.

**Interpretation:** Reported difficulty cannot be attributed solely to infrequent practice. Many respondents practice regularly but still perceive Calculus as challenging. This raises questions about other possible factors, including conceptual understanding, problem-solving skills, mathematical anxiety, and the effectiveness of their learning strategies. However, these results alone cannot establish which factors cause the reported difficulties.

### Q6. Which topic do you find most difficult?

*Respondents could select multiple topics.*

- **Limits and Continuity:** 10 students (15.4%)
- **Differentiation Rules:** 24 students (36.9%)
- **Applications of Differentiation:** 27 students (41.5%)
- **Indefinite Integration:** 24 students (36.9%)
- **Definite Integration:** 25 students (38.5%)
- **Applications of Integration (Area Under a Curve):** 20 students (30.8%)

**Observation:** Applications of Differentiation was the most frequently selected difficult topic (41.5%), followed by Definite Integration (38.5%).

**Interpretation:** Application-based topics appear particularly challenging for respondents. While 15.4% selected Limits and Continuity, larger proportions identified several differentiation and integration topics as difficult. This pattern may indicate that applying mathematical concepts and rules to solve problems presents a greater challenge than introductory topics. Further investigation would be needed to determine why these topics are perceived as difficult.

## 3. Reasons for Difficulty and Learning Strategies

### Q7. What is the biggest reason Calculus feels difficult to you?

*Respondents could select multiple reasons.*

- **My basic mathematical skills are not strong enough:** 11 students (16.9%)
- **I struggle to understand the concepts:** 16 students (24.6%)
- **There are too many formulas and rules to remember:** 35 students (53.8%)
- **I do not get enough practice:** 15 students (23.1%)
- **My textbook or teacher's explanations are difficult to follow:** 17 students (26.2%)
- **I feel anxious when solving mathematics problems:** 19 students (29.2%)
- **I struggle with Trigonometry and Trigonometric identities in Calculus:** 15 students (23.1%)
- **I have not studied enough Calculus yet:** 7 students (10.8%)

**Observation:** The most frequently selected reason was the perceived number of formulas and rules, reported by 53.8% of respondents. Mathematical anxiety (29.2%) and difficulty following explanations (26.2%) were also commonly reported.

**Interpretation:** The findings suggest that students perceive Calculus difficulty as involving several factors, including formula overload, anxiety, conceptual understanding, and instructional clarity. The prominence of formula overload is particularly noteworthy because it can be compared with students' responses about their actual problem-solving difficulties in Q10.

### Q8. Which of the following strategies do you use to learn Calculus?

*Respondents could select multiple strategies.*

- **I focus on understanding concepts deeply before solving problems:** 19 students (29.2%)
- **I memorize formulas and practice problems:** 31 students (47.7%)
- **I rely mainly on teachers/coaching classes and notes:** 23 students (35.4%)
- **I memorize standard rules and shortcut techniques:** 12 students (18.5%)
- **I do not follow any specific strategy:** 6 students (9.2%)

**Observation:** Memorizing formulas and practicing problems was the most frequently selected learning strategy, reported by 47.7% of respondents.

**Interpretation:** Many respondents combine memorization with problem practice, while fewer reported prioritizing deep conceptual understanding. When considered alongside Q7, this pattern raises the possibility that students may rely on memorization while simultaneously feeling overwhelmed by formulas. However, because the responses are aggregated, it is not possible to determine whether the same individuals selected both options or whether memorization directly contributes to their difficulties.

### Q9. Which resources do you use most often to learn Calculus?

*Respondents could select multiple resources.*

- **Textbooks:** 25 students (38.5%)
- **Class notes:** 30 students (46.2%)
- **Online resources (YouTube, websites, recorded lectures):** 24 students (36.9%)
- **Friends or classmates:** 5 students (7.7%)
- **Home tutor:** 28 students (43.1%)
- **Coaching classes:** 14 students (21.5%)
- **I mostly study on my own:** 12 students (18.5%)
- **Other:** 0 students (0%)

**Observation:** Class notes (46.2%), home tutors (43.1%), and textbooks (38.5%) were among the most frequently selected learning resources. Online resources were also commonly used (36.9%).

**Interpretation:** Respondents reported using a combination of classroom materials, private tutoring, textbooks, and online resources. Relatively few selected friends or classmates as a learning resource. These results describe reported resource usage but do not establish whether any particular resource is more effective than another.

## 4. Challenges During Problem-Solving

### Q10. Which part of solving a Calculus problem do you find most challenging?

- **Understanding what the question is asking:** 16 students (24.6%)
- **Identifying the correct Calculus concept or rule to use:** 19 students (29.2%)
- **Applying Algebra or Trigonometry while solving the problem:** 19 students (29.2%)
- **Performing calculations correctly:** 24 students (36.9%)
- **Applying the correct method step by step:** 20 students (30.8%)
- **Remembering formulas:** 1 student (1.5%)

**Observation:** Performing calculations correctly was the most frequently selected challenge (36.9%). Applying the correct method step by step followed at 30.8%. Only one respondent (1.5%) selected remembering formulas as the most challenging part.

**Interpretation:** An important distinction emerges between students' general perceptions of Calculus difficulty and the specific difficulties they encounter while solving problems. Although formula overload was the most frequently selected reason in Q7, very few respondents selected formula recall as their primary problem-solving challenge in Q10.

Instead, respondents more frequently identified calculation accuracy, method selection, and step-by-step application as challenges. This suggests that students' difficulties may extend beyond remembering formulas to include using mathematical knowledge effectively during problem-solving.

## 5. Key Finding: Perceived Formula Overload vs. Problem-Solving Difficulties

One of the most notable findings is the difference between responses to Q7 and Q10.

- **53.8%** of respondents identified the number of formulas and rules as a reason Calculus feels difficult.
- **1.5%** identified remembering formulas as their most challenging part of solving a Calculus problem.
- **36.9%** identified performing calculations correctly as a major problem-solving challenge.
- **30.8%** identified applying the correct method step by step as a challenge.
- **29.2%** identified selecting the appropriate Calculus concept or rule as a challenge.

This contrast suggests that students may perceive formula overload as a major source of difficulty, even though they identify other tasks as more challenging when solving individual problems.

One possible explanation is that students associate the difficulty of Calculus with the number of rules they must learn, while their difficulties during problem-solving may involve calculation accuracy, choosing an appropriate method, or applying concepts correctly.

However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Q7 asked respondents to identify reasons Calculus feels difficult, whereas Q10 asked them to select the most challenging part of solving a problem. These questions measure different aspects of the learning experience and are not directly equivalent. Furthermore, the aggregated results do not reveal whether individual students who selected formula overload in Q7 selected calculation or method application in Q10.

Therefore, the results highlight a potentially meaningful pattern rather than proving that formula overload is merely a perceived problem or that problem-solving difficulties are its underlying cause.

## 6. Qualitative Insights from Open-Ended Responses

### Q11. Is there anything else you would like to share about your experience learning Calculus?

Selected responses included:

- "I think I didn’t learn Trigonometry before and this is why Calculus seems hard to me."
- "When I study Calculus, it feels horrible like a nightmare."
- "The more I practice, the easier it gets."
- "I HATE CALCULUS!"

**Observation:** The responses reflected a range of experiences, including frustration, anxiety, difficulties with prerequisite knowledge, and optimism about improvement through practice.

**Interpretation:** These comments provide additional context for the quantitative findings. One respondent connected Calculus difficulty with a lack of prior Trigonometry knowledge, consistent with the responses to Q7 about difficulties with Trigonometry. Other comments reflected strong negative emotions, while one respondent described practice as a way to improve.

Although these responses illustrate different student experiences, the selected quotations should not be treated as representative of all respondents. They provide qualitative examples that help contextualize the survey results rather than establish how common each experience is.

## 7. Overall Conclusion

The survey explored how students perceive Calculus difficulty, the learning strategies and resources they use, and the challenges they encounter while solving problems.

Three patterns stand out:

1. **Calculus is widely perceived as challenging.** A total of 56.9% of respondents rated its difficulty as 4 or 5 on the five-point scale.

2. **Formula overload is a prominent concern.** More than half of the respondents (53.8%) selected the number of formulas and rules as a reason Calculus feels difficult. Memorizing formulas while practicing problems was also the most frequently selected learning strategy (47.7%).

3. **Problem-solving involves challenges beyond formula recall.** Respondents more frequently selected calculation accuracy (36.9%), step-by-step method application (30.8%), and identifying appropriate concepts or rules (29.2%) than remembering formulas (1.5%) as their main problem-solving challenge.

Taken together, these findings suggest that understanding students' difficulties with Calculus requires examining more than their ability to remember formulas. Their experiences may also involve mathematical foundations, conceptual understanding, calculation accuracy, problem-solving approaches, and emotional responses to mathematics.

However, this study has important limitations. The survey included 65 respondents, most of whom were Class 11 or Class 12 students who had chosen Higher Mathematics. The findings therefore should not be generalized to all students studying Calculus. In addition, the survey measured self-reported experiences rather than directly assessing mathematical ability or learning outcomes. Because the data are observational and descriptive, the results cannot establish causal relationships between learning strategies and perceived difficulty.

Despite these limitations, the survey provides an initial picture of how a group of Bangladeshi students experience Calculus and identifies several areas that could be explored in future research, particularly the relationship between formula-focused learning, conceptual understanding, and problem-solving difficulties.
